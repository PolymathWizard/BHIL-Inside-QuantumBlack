# Exhibit grammar

The exhibit is the primary reasoning unit. Each one makes a single point
and shows the evidence cleanly. This chapter is the anatomy lesson.

## The five parts of a well-formed exhibit

1. **Full-sentence conclusion title.** States the finding, not the chart
   type. Never a label.
2. **Single dominant comparison.** One visual contrast carries the point.
   Everything else recedes.
3. **Direct labels or a simple legend.** Direct labels where they reduce
   scan time; otherwise a minimal legend.
4. **Disciplined source line.** Source, sample, and qualifiers stay
   visible beneath the evidence.
5. **Method note when needed.** Define filters or unusual calculations
   where a careful reader would ask.

## Visual rules that hold across the corpus

* **Sequential numbering.** Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2, and so on, in order
  across the report, so exhibits can be cited from the text.
* **Restrained palette.** Two or three data colors plus neutrals.
* **Clarity over ornament.** Remove anything that does not carry meaning.
* **Annotation only when useful.** Callouts add insight; they do not
  restate what the bars already show.

## The read-the-titles-only test

Open any flagship survey report and read nothing but exhibit titles. If
the sequence tells the whole argument, the grammar is working. This is
the acceptance test the BHIL build checklist borrows for every artifact.

## Common failure modes the grammar prevents

* A chart that needs its caption to make sense.
* A legend the reader must memorize before the bars mean anything.
* Two findings fighting inside one visual.
* A number with no visible source, which reads as a claim, not evidence.
