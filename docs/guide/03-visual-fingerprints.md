# Five visual fingerprints

The look is restrained, but the system is highly repeatable. Five rules
account for most of what a reader experiences as "polish."

## 1. Conclusion-led titles

Exhibit titles state the finding as a sentence rather than naming the
chart. "Adoption grows while measured impact remains limited" is a title;
"Adoption by year" is a label. The corpus uses titles.

## 2. One idea per exhibit

Each chart or model carries one dominant comparison or decision point.
When a second idea appears, it gets a second exhibit. This is the single
highest-leverage rule for a designer to adopt.

## 3. Visible sourcing

Source, sample, date, and method notes stay visually quiet but present,
usually a small line beneath the evidence. Quiet is not optional; absent
is not allowed.

## 4. Limited data palette

Most charts work with two or three data colors plus neutral gray. Color
marks the argument, not the categories. The emphasis color lands on the
series the title is about.

## 5. White space as control

Large margins, light rules, and short text blocks keep attention on the
evidence. Density is achieved through exhibit count, not through cramming.

## The STENCIL observation

The design system is less about decoration and more about control: one
claim, one exhibit, one source trail. Tier: CORROBORATED across the T1
corpus; the census behind it is summarized in
[exhibit grammar](04-exhibit-grammar.md).
